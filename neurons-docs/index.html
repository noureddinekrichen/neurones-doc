Application Monitoring Guide

What Neurons monitors, how to read its screens, and how to use it day to day — from the first glance at your services to administering the platform.

Introduction

What is Neurons?

Neurons is an Application Performance Monitoring (APM) platform. It automatically collects the traces, logs, and performance measurements emitted by your applications, stores them in a single data warehouse, and turns them into actionable dashboards: service health, business transactions, end-user experience, and alerts.

The goal is to answer three questions that any team running an application asks constantly: is everything working normally right now, what changed when a problem appeared, and how far back can I go to understand a past incident.

How data arrives in the platform

Applications, websites, and mobile apps are instrumented with OpenTelemetry, an open standard. This instrumentation continuously sends what is happening to a collector (the OpenTelemetry Collector), which then forwards this data to Neurons.

  1. Applications and browsers generate traces (the step-by-step detail of a request) and logs.
  2. The OpenTelemetry Collector gathers them and forwards them to the Neurons ingestion service.
  3. The ingestion service validates, cleans, and stores this data almost as-is in the data warehouse — very little transformation happens at this stage, to avoid losing anything and to stay fast.
  4. Background jobs (workers) then continuously read this data back to compute everything shown on the dashboards: request rate, error rate, response time, the service dependency map, and business transactions.
  5. Dashboards query these already-computed results instead of recalculating on every view, which keeps them fast even over long time ranges.

Nearly all the curves and trends shown on screen (traffic, latency, errors over time) come from these pre-aggregated computations, not from recalculating raw data every time a screen opens. That's what keeps the platform responsive even with months of history.

Key concepts

A few terms recur throughout the platform and are worth telling apart before browsing the screens:

TermMeaning

Application Monitoring

This part covers the screens used day to day to monitor application behavior. These are the functional equivalent of what other APM platforms call "Application Monitoring".

Applications — overview

The "Applications" screen is the home page of the APM module. It lists every application that has recently emitted telemetry, along with its number of distinct services and an overall health indicator. It's the starting point for quickly spotting which application deserves your attention before diving into detail.

If no application appears, the most common cause is that no recent telemetry has been received: widen the time window, or check that the OpenTelemetry Collector is actually sending its data to Neurons.

Services and dependency map

The "Services" screen lists every active service with its RED indicators (rate, errors, latency) over the selected time window, along with a live throughput chart and an automatically generated health summary. It's the most-visited screen day to day.

The service dependency map (Service Map) is built into this screen: it visually shows which service calls which other, with each link carrying a health indicator (healthy, degraded, critical) based on that call's error rate. Each service can also be opened individually to show:

  • the trend of requests per minute,
  • the trend of errors per minute,
  • latency percentiles (p50, p95, p99…),
  • this service's own database calls (call count, total time, average time),
  • detail per operation (endpoint), with its own RED indicators,
  • an AI-generated health analysis that summarizes in natural language what's happening on the service.

Traces

The "Traces" screen lets you search and inspect individual requests rather than aggregates. Each trace is reconstructed as an enriched service graph that distinguishes the types of calls encountered: calls between internal services, database calls, cache, messaging, RPC calls, external providers, and browser-side (frontend) steps.

This is the screen to use to understand precisely why a given request was slow or failed: you see the exact sequence of steps, their individual duration, and where the time was actually spent.

Business Transactions

A business transaction is a named, tracked-over-time application entry point — for example "Login", "Product search", or "Checkout". Unlike services, business transactions aren't declared manually: they are automatically detected by a background job that spots recurring traffic patterns, then registered in a catalog.

For each business transaction, the platform lets you view its own health history, its flow map (the services traversed by traces that start with this transaction), and configure dedicated notifications.

Detection rules and groups

Detection rules let you refine or prioritize how traffic is recognized as belonging to one transaction or another, and groups let you control how several related transactions are counted together. A business transaction can also be manually removed (sent to trash) with a configurable grace period before permanent deletion.

Business Journeys

Business Journeys are conversion funnels that link several steps together — for example: product page → add to cart → checkout → confirmation. They are reconstructed by combining browser-side page views (RUM) with the associated business transactions, which shows, step by step, exactly where users actually abandon their journey.

Errors

The "Errors" screen groups failing traces by signature — meaning by similar error type — rather than listing every occurrence individually. This lets you immediately see which errors are the most frequent over the period, then open a representative example of each signature to investigate it.

Database

The "Database" screen groups all outbound database calls by normalized query signature (the same query with different parameters counts as a single signature). It shows call volume, total time spent, and average time per call, which makes it easy to quickly spot the most expensive queries.

Extended Observability

Beyond application monitoring itself, Neurons also covers application logs, real end-user experience, and a dedicated workspace for cross-cutting incident investigation.

Logs

The "Logs" screen gives access to recent application logs, filterable by severity level (info, warning, error, etc.) and by time window. It's designed to be used alongside traces: a trace shows how a request unfolded, logs provide the textual detail the application chose to write at a given moment.

Events

The "Observability Event Center" centralizes every event generated by the platform: a threshold breach on a service, a health rule violation, etc. Each event has a severity and follows an explicit lifecycle:

  • Open — the event was just detected and hasn't been handled yet.
  • Acknowledged — someone has taken ownership of the investigation.
  • Resolved — the problem is fixed; only from this point does an event become eligible for automatic purging.
  • Suppressed — the event is explicitly set aside, for example because it's known and has no impact.

An open or acknowledged event is never automatically purged, no matter its age — only the retention of resolved events is configurable.

User Experience (RUM)

This screen, labeled "Browser Performance" in the interface, measures the real experience of a web application's visitors (Real User Monitoring). It combines several types of analysis:

  • Web Vitals — the standard perceived-performance indicators: LCP, INP, CLS, FCP, and TTFB, with percentiles and a good / needs improvement / poor breakdown.
  • Load time distribution — a histogram of page load durations with percentile markers.
  • Sessions and replay — every user session can be reconstructed as a chronological journey, and some sessions have a replayable recording.
  • Frustration signals — automatic detection of rage clicks, dead clicks, and error clicks, which reveal a degraded experience even without a visible technical error.
  • Geographic and device breakdown — browser, operating system, device, or geographic region.
  • Correlation with business transactions — the ability to see which backend business transactions are triggered by a given page, and vice versa.

Troubleshoot

The "Troubleshoot" screen is a cross-cutting workspace for investigating an ongoing problem, organized into four tabs:

TabWhat it shows
Slow Response TimesBreakdown of requests by latency bucket (normal, slow, very slow, stalled) and the main contributors to those slowdowns.
ErrorsThe dominant error signatures over the selected window, with their trend.
Health Rule ViolationsHealth rules currently in violation, with the context needed to investigate.
War RoomsManual tracking of open incidents, with AI-generated postmortem reports.

A "comparison" mode lets you contrast the current time window against the equivalent previous window, which helps distinguish normal variation from a genuine degradation. Any identified item can be opened directly in the Traces, Errors, or Logs screens, already filtered to the right context.

Alerting & Notifications

The Alerting module brings together everything that lets the platform warn a team rather than just display numbers: threshold definitions, notification channels, and manual incident tracking. The "Alerting" screen is organized into six tabs: Overview, Rules, Alerts, Incidents, Policies, Destinations, Templates.

Health Rules

A health rule defines a threshold on an indicator (for example: error rate above 20%, or latency above 1500 ms), evaluated over a given scope (application, environment, service). When a rule is violated, it generates an event, visible both in the Events screen and in the Troubleshoot "Health Rule Violations" tab.

To avoid false alarms on anecdotal traffic, a minimum request-volume threshold is applied before a latency or error-rate rule is evaluated.

Policies, destinations, and templates

Three building blocks work together to turn an event into an actual notification:

  • Destinations — the channels a notification can be sent to: email, Slack webhook, Microsoft Teams webhook, generic webhook, or a REST API call. Each destination can be tested before being used in production.
  • Templates — a notification's content and formatting, reusable across policies.
  • Policies — the rule that links a trigger condition to one or more destinations and a template.

Incidents and War Rooms

An incident can be opened manually as soon as a team starts investigating a problem, independently of automatic events. It groups the affected services, a status (open / resolved), and free-form notes. The Troubleshoot "War Rooms" tab is where these incidents are tracked, and it offers AI-generated postmortem report generation once the incident is resolved.

Administration & Operations

This part is for the people responsible for the platform's own proper operation: configuration, data retention, application instrumentation, external integrations, deployment, and backup.

Data Retention

Data retention determines how long each telemetry category is kept before automatic deletion — a trade-off between storage cost and how far back you can investigate. It's configured separately for each category, since their usefulness over time isn't the same:

CategoryDefaultRangeWhy
Spans (raw traces)30 d1–90 dHigh-volume; mostly useful in the first days/weeks to investigate a specific incident.
Logs30 d1–90 dSame profile as raw traces.
Metrics — 1 minute15 d1–30 dFine-grained analysis, used beyond a week during an in-depth investigation.
Metrics — 1 hour90 d7–180 dMonth-over-month trend comparisons.
Metrics — 1 day400 d30–730 dYear-over-year comparisons.
Resolved events90 d1–730 dOpen or acknowledged events are never purged, no matter their age.

For each area, the page shows a plain-language description, a real usage indicator (row count and estimated size), the day-count setting, an immediate-purge button (with confirmation), and a history of past purges.

Disk size is an estimate; if it isn't available for a table, the row count is still shown, with an explicit note that the size is unknown rather than showing a false value.

An automatic purge runs once a day and applies the settings configured for each area. A purge can also be triggered manually at any time; either way, the action is logged with the date, the number of rows deleted, and the trigger source.

Application Instrumentation (OpenTelemetry)

For an application to appear in Neurons, it must be instrumented with OpenTelemetry and configured to export its traces and logs to the Collector. This section covers a common point of attention once basic instrumentation is in place: capturing HTTP headers and, more rarely, request/response bodies.

Capturing HTTP headers

For security, OpenTelemetry captures no HTTP headers by default — headers frequently contain sensitive information (tokens, session cookies). Capture must be explicitly enabled, separately for inbound and outbound requests, and separately for the request and the response. This is done via environment variables for most backend languages; on the browser side, the same explicit list is declared in the instrumentation's JavaScript configuration.

Whatever the language, the same rule applies: never capture "all headers" — always use an explicit list, and always exclude authentication headers and cookies.

Capturing request and response bodies

OpenTelemetry doesn't natively capture request or response content (the body) — only their size in bytes. Capturing the actual content requires custom instrumentation, enabled only for explicitly chosen routes, with two rules to always follow: limit the captured size, and mask sensitive fields before sending anything — there's no undoing it once the data has been exported.

External Integrations

Two integrations connect Neurons to the rest of the operations ecosystem:

  • External CMDB — synchronizes the consolidated list of applications, services, and business transactions with an external configuration management database.
  • External event management — forwards the events generated by Neurons to an external incident management tool.

Kubernetes Deployment

Neurons deploys as three independent components, each with its own image, grouped in the same Kubernetes namespace:

ComponentRoleScaling
apm-ingestReceives telemetry, runs the compute workers, and serves the query API.The ingestion/query service scales horizontally; the 3 background workers stay single-instance.
neurones-backendControl API: authentication, CMDB, Collector administration, real-time events.Single instance.
neurones-frontendWeb application (dashboards, topology, settings), Next.js.Scales horizontally.

All three components rely on an external Vertica database and a small shared PostgreSQL control database. Both must exist and be reachable before starting the deployment.

Things to watch before a first deployment

  • A StorageClass supporting ReadWriteMany access is required for the shared GeoIP database volume — many default cloud storage classes (EBS, for example) don't allow it.
  • The GeoIP database (MaxMind GeoLite2) isn't shipped in the published image for licensing reasons: each customer must generate their own license key.
  • The neurones-backend PostgreSQL schema isn't applied automatically in production — an alembic upgrade head migration must be run explicitly before the first startup.
  • The Vertica schema isn't applied automatically either: all migrations must be applied before apm-ingest's first startup.
  • neurones-backend only accepts origins explicitly listed in its CORS configuration; forgetting this results in a frontend that renders but whose API calls all fail silently.
  • neurones-frontend's public URLs (API and WebSocket) are baked in at image build time, not at container startup.
  • The ingestion buffer is in-memory only, with no durable queue behind it: size memory with headroom, and allow enough shutdown grace time during updates.
  • The ingestion token is a single shared secret, not scoped per customer: treat it as sensitive and rotate it if it's ever exposed.

Once configured, a single script (deploy-neurons-apm.sh) handles the entire deployment, then waits until everything is ready. It can safely be re-run after a configuration change.

Backup and Restore

All of Neurons's data lives in a single Vertica database, called the "Data Lake". Protecting this one database means protecting the entire system — no other copy exists anywhere else.

What to know before trusting a backup

  • Data "in flight" isn't covered. Telemetry received but not yet written to the database is lost if the ingestion service stops abruptly.
  • The restore point is that of the last successful backup. Anything ingested between the last backup and the incident is permanently lost.

Three destination types are supported: S3-compatible object storage (recommended in production), an NFS network share, or local disk.

Restore procedure, in brief

  1. Assess the extent of the damage — when in doubt, treat it as total loss.
  2. Stop the ingestion service and all background workers before restoring anything.
  3. Restore the last valid backup (or a specific named backup).
  4. Validate the restored database before reopening traffic.
  5. Restart the ingestion service and the workers — each automatically resumes from its own checkpoint.
  6. Rebuild whatever can be rebuilt for the lost period (dependency graph, business transaction registry).
  7. Clearly communicate the affected time window.

A backup that has never been restored is not a verified backup. A monthly test restore, and a full drill at least once a year, are recommended.
